# Crawl frontier prioritisation algorithms: ordering newly discovered, not-yet-fetched items

Scope: which algorithms decide what to fetch first when the frontier (~hundreds of thousands of Telegram handles) is far larger than the budget (~80k probes/day shared with refresh). Our data: P(live public channel) by reference kind is forward 85%, reply 81%, link 59%, mention-only 26%; by distinct citers it is 1 citer 43%, 2-4 60%, 5-19 73%, 20+ 78%; handles ending in "bot" are 99.7% dead; 64% of found targets are cited by Russian channels while the deployment follows Persian/English.

Primary texts I read in full (via pdftotext): Abiteboul et al. 2003 (OPIC), Baeza-Yates et al. 2005, Gouriten et al. 2014, Olston & Najork 2010 survey, Aggarwal et al. 2001 (HTML), Hersovici et al. 1998 (HTML). Everything else is from abstracts or secondary summaries and is marked that way.

## 1. OPIC (Abiteboul, Preda, Cobena, WWW 2003): cash/history, formulas, ordering, convergence, weaknesses

### Takeaway
OPIC is a one-pass, matrix-free PageRank estimator. Each fetched node pays its accumulated "cash" equally to its out-links, and a frontier node's priority is the cash it has received so far. That makes it a *weighted backlink count* computable incrementally. In simulation it matched larger-sites-first and beat breadth-first. Its weak spot is the start of a crawl, before much link evidence exists, and it is topic-blind.

### Cited Findings
- Algorithm (exact, from the paper): initialise `C[i] := 1/n`, `H[i] := 0`, `G := 0`. Loop forever: choose some node i (every node must be chosen infinitely often); `H[i] += C[i]`; for each child j of i, `C[j] += C[i]/out[i]`; `G += C[i]`; `C[i] := 0`. Importance estimate of page k at any step is `(H[k] + C[k]) / (G + 1)`. — [Abiteboul et al. 2003, PDF](https://cs.brynmawr.edu/Courses/cs380/fall2006/online_page_importance.pdf)
- Invariants and convergence: total cash is constant (`sum_i C_t[i] = 1`, Lemma 2.2); if every page is read infinitely often then `|H_t| -> infinity`; Theorem 2.1 says `X_t = H_t/|H_t|` converges to the importance vector, and the error is bounded by `1/|H_t|` (they call `1/G` the "error factor"). The order of visits does not affect correctness, only speed. — [Abiteboul et al. 2003](https://cs.brynmawr.edu/Courses/cs380/fall2006/online_page_importance.pdf)
- Strong connectivity is enforced with a virtual page: every node links to it, and it links to all nodes. Its cash is not distributed all at once, and it is the mechanism for handling new pages. — [Abiteboul et al. 2003](https://cs.brynmawr.edu/Courses/cs380/fall2006/online_page_importance.pdf)
- Three selection policies were compared: Random, Greedy (crawl the highest-cash page next) and Cycle. Analytically, Greedy reads pages that carry on average `2/n` cash against `1/n` for Random, so "the error factor decreases on average twice faster with Greedy than with Random". On synthetic power-law graphs (N = 100,000), Greedy and Cycle had about equal error over all pages and Random was "significantly worse". On the top 10% most important pages, Greedy converged faster than every other policy, the off-line power iteration included. — [Abiteboul et al. 2003](https://cs.brynmawr.edu/Courses/cs380/fall2006/online_page_importance.pdf)
- Adaptive OPIC, for changing graphs, uses only a recent window of history. There are three window policies: Fixed Window (time T), Variable Window (the last k crawls), and Interpolation, which stores two numbers per page and is the one Xyleme used. Interpolation update: `H[i] := H[i]*(T - (G - G[i]))/T + C[i]` if `G - G[i] < T`, else `H[i] := C[i]*T/(G - G[i])`. Small windows react faster but are less precise. — [Abiteboul et al. 2003](https://cs.brynmawr.edu/Courses/cs380/fall2006/online_page_importance.pdf)
- New pages get "a default history that corresponds to the importance of recently introduced pages", on the stated reasoning that "important pages are discovered first, whereas new or recently introduced pages are often the least important ones". — [Abiteboul et al. 2003](https://cs.brynmawr.edu/Courses/cs380/fall2006/online_page_importance.pdf)
- Cash can be steered. For news detection, pages "suspected to contain news wires (e.g. because the URL contains 'news') receive some 'extra' cash", taken from the virtual page so the total stays constant. They also mention anchor-text semantics as a future criterion. — [Abiteboul et al. 2003](https://cs.brynmawr.edu/Courses/cs380/fall2006/online_page_importance.pdf)
- A variant ran in production at Xyleme, where each crawler fetched "millions of pages per day". The paper says that after 400M pages crawled it had "a relatively precise approximation of page importance for over 1 billion pages". — [Abiteboul et al. 2003](https://cs.brynmawr.edu/Courses/cs380/fall2006/online_page_importance.pdf)
- In the crawl-ordering comparison, OPIC is described as "a weighted backlink-count strategy … similar to Pagerank, but has no random links and the calculation is not iterative". It "has a bad performance at the beginning of the crawl, but it improves as more link information is gathered". — [Baeza-Yates et al. 2005](https://archives.iw3c2.org/www2005/proceedings/docs/p864.pdf)
- In Gouriten et al.'s focused-crawling benchmark, OPIC as a frontier estimator, `beta(v) = (H(v)+C(v))/(G+1)`, scored 0.28 / 0.18 / 0.21 / 0.29 of the oracle-greedy score at 100 / 1k / 10k / 100k steps. Simple neighbourhood heuristics scored 0.38 to 0.59. The authors' summary is "bfs and opic are significantly worse than the rest", because OPIC "is PageRank specific and initially does not take into account node and edge weights". — [Gouriten et al. 2014](https://www.cl.cam.ac.uk/~ey204/teaching/ACS/R212_2015_2016/papers/gouriten_HT_2014.pdf)
- Apache Nutch ships an OPIC scoring plugin based on this paper. — [Nutch OPIC javadoc](https://nutch.apache.org/documentation/javadoc/api/org/apache/nutch/scoring/opic/package-summary.html)

### Inferences
- For handle discovery, OPIC means each probed or synced channel distributes a unit of cash across the distinct handles it references. A handle cited by many *important* channels rises, and a handle cited 50 times by one spam channel does not. This is the "distinct citers" signal weighted by citer importance.
- OPIC's weaknesses all apply here. It is topic-blind, so cash from Russian citers counts the same as cash from Persian ones. It ignores reference kind. It is weak early, and our frontier is "early" for any newly discovered region. The fix the paper itself suggests, injecting extra cash by feature, is the bridge to Section 3's learned scores. A cleaner design: priority = P(live and relevant | features) as in Aggarwal, with OPIC-style cash as one feature.
- One adaptation is to distribute cash only from channels the deployment cares about (Persian/English-followed channels, or channels already judged relevant). That makes it a topic-sensitive / personalised OPIC. This is my inference; I found no paper that evaluates it.

### Gaps
- The paper reports no harvest-style numbers for OPIC as a *discovery ordering* in its own right, only convergence of the importance estimate. Discovery-ordering numbers come from Baeza-Yates 2005 and Gouriten 2014.

## 2. Crawl-ordering studies: Cho et al. 1998, Najork & Wiener 2001, Baeza-Yates et al. 2005, partial PageRank, backlink count

### Takeaway
For importance-weighted coverage with no topic, the ranking is roughly: historical PageRank > OPIC ≈ larger-sites-first ≈ batch PageRank > breadth-first > random > backlink-count ≈ partial PageRank. Plain backlink count ranked *below random* in the largest controlled comparison because it gets stuck in locally dense regions.

### Cited Findings
- Cho, Garcia-Molina & Page (1998) crawled 179,000 stanford.edu pages virtually and compared breadth-first, backlink count, PageRank and random against "hot" pages, defined by backlinks or PageRank. PageRank ordering worked best and breadth-first "works almost as well", in particular when hot pages are defined by PageRank. — [Cho et al. 1998, WWW7](https://archives.iw3c2.org/www7/proceedings/1919/com1919.htm) (summary via search result; full paper not read)
- Najork & Wiener (2001) crawled 328M unique pages and found that breadth-first order discovers high-PageRank pages early. A secondary source notes they did not compare against other strategies. — [Najork & Wiener 2001](https://archives.iw3c2.org/www10/cdrom/papers/208/index.html); [Wikipedia: Web crawler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Web_crawler)
- Baeza-Yates, Castillo, Marín & Rodríguez (WWW 2005) simulated crawls on .cl and .gr, two complete crawls each of about 2.4 to 3.6M pages with a 25,000 page/site cap. They compared breadth-first, backlink-count, batch-PageRank (recomputed every K = 100,000 pages), partial-PageRank, OPIC, larger-sites-first, four historical-PageRank variants and an omniscient oracle. — [Baeza-Yates et al. 2005](https://archives.iw3c2.org/www2005/proceedings/docs/p864.pdf)
- Definitions from that paper. Partial-PageRank gives new pages a temporary score of "the sum of the Pagerank of the pages pointing to it divided by the number of out-links of those pages". Larger-sites-first "uses the number of un-crawled pages found so far as the priority for picking a Web site". Historical-pagerank-parent assigns a new page "the Pagerank of the parent page … divided by the number of out-links of the parent page". — [Baeza-Yates et al. 2005](https://archives.iw3c2.org/www2005/proceedings/docs/p864.pdf)
- Average cumulative PageRank over the crawl (Table 2, mean of 4 collections; random would be 0.5): backlink-count 0.50, partial-PageRank 0.52, breadth-first 0.64, batch-PageRank 0.63, OPIC 0.67, larger-sites-first 0.67, hist-pr-zero 0.68, hist-pr-random 0.70, hist-pr-parent 0.70, hist-pr-omniscient 0.77, omniscient 0.74. Kendall's τ against the optimal order: backlink 0.016, partial-PR 0.024, BFS 0.129, batch-PR 0.196, OPIC 0.223, larger-sites-first 0.250, historical 0.35 to 0.37. — [Baeza-Yates et al. 2005](https://archives.iw3c2.org/www2005/proceedings/docs/p864.pdf)
- From the same paper: "backlink-count and partial-pagerank are the worst strategy … and perform [worse] than a random crawl. They both tend to get stuck in pages that are locally optimal". Breadth-first is "close to the best strategies for the first 20-30% of pages" and then degrades. OPIC, batch-PageRank and larger-sites-first "can retrieve about half of the Pagerank value … downloading only around 20-30% of the pages". The omniscient strategy is "too greedy" and performs close to random late in the crawl. — [Baeza-Yates et al. 2005](https://archives.iw3c2.org/www2005/proceedings/docs/p864.pdf)
- On historical information, a PageRank four months old was usable for only 55% of pages, but those pages held 72% of September's PageRank. History-guided strategies beat OPIC "mostly because of OPIC's poorer performance at the beginning of the crawl" and improve on OPIC and larger-sites-first by "at most 3%". With no history, the authors recommend OPIC or larger-sites-first, and prefer larger-sites-first for distributed crawlers because it needs no weighted in-link counts. — [Baeza-Yates et al. 2005](https://archives.iw3c2.org/www2005/proceedings/docs/p864.pdf)
- Boldi et al. showed that PageRank approximations computed on partial graphs "can be very inexact" (as cited by Baeza-Yates et al.). — [Baeza-Yates et al. 2005](https://archives.iw3c2.org/www2005/proceedings/docs/p864.pdf)
- Survey-level guarantee: for PageRank biased to a trusted seed set T, "a breadth-first crawl of depth d using T as its seed set achieves WC ≥ 1 − α^(d+1)" (α is the damping factor, Cho & Schonfeld). Variants that count only *external* (cross-site) links are recommended as spam-resistant. — [Olston & Najork 2010](https://khoury.northeastern.edu/home/vip/teach/IRcourse/IR_surveys/olston-najork@web-crawling10.pdf)
- Fetterly et al. compared BFS, in-degree, trans-domain in-degree and PageRank orderings on search-relevance metrics (MaxNDCG, click count). PageRank was "the most reliable and effective", and "imposing per-domain page limits boosts effectiveness". — [Olston & Najork 2010](https://khoury.northeastern.edu/home/vip/teach/IRcourse/IR_surveys/olston-najork@web-crawling10.pdf)

### Inferences
- Our "distinct citing channels" count is the analogue of *trans-domain in-degree*, since each Telegram channel is its own "site". That is the spam-resistant form of backlink count, and our data already shows it predicts liveness monotonically (43% to 78%). Raw reference count, meaning repeated mentions from the same channel, is the plain backlink count that Baeza-Yates found worse than random. Do not use it.
- "Historical" information is available to us: past probe outcomes per handle, per citer and per feature bucket. Baeza-Yates found history was the best non-oracle signal, which argues for learning P(live) from our own probe log rather than from graph structure alone.
- Larger-sites-first maps naturally to "citer with the most un-probed outgoing references first". It amounts to draining sources fairly, not to a value estimate. I treat it as a fairness/throughput device (Section 7), not a priority score.

### Gaps
- I did not read the Cho 1998 or Najork & Wiener full texts. Their numbers above come from abstracts and secondary summaries.
- I did not locate the Boldi et al. ("Do your worst to make the best", WWW 2004) paper directly.

## 3. Focused crawling, and combining independent signals into P(relevant): Chakrabarti, Shark-search, Diligenti, Menczer/Pant, Aggarwal

### Takeaway
Aggarwal et al.'s "intelligent crawling" gives a ready formula for combining kind, citer count and citer language. Each evidence E contributes an interest ratio `I(C,E) = P(C|E)/P(C)`. Ratios multiply under independence, so priority is a weighted sum of log interest ratios. This is naive-Bayes log-odds. Shark-search adds a decayed inherited score so the crawler keeps exploring past irrelevant intermediaries. The focused-crawling literature agrees that being slightly less greedy helps long runs.

### Cited Findings
- **Aggarwal, Al-Garawi & Yu, WWW10 2001.** Interest ratio `I(C,E) = P(C|E)/P(C) = P(C∩E)/(P(C)P(E))`. A value above 1 is evidence for, below 1 is evidence against. With several evidences, the composite is the product `∏ I(C,E_i)` under an independence assumption. — [Aggarwal et al. 2001](https://archives.iw3c2.org/www10/cdrom/papers/110/index.html)
- Four evidence families. Content words of in-linking pages give `I_c = ∏ I(C,Q_i)`. URL tokens, split on "." and "/", give `I_u = ∏ I(C,R_i)`. The link signal is `I_l = p^m · q^(k−m)`, where k crawled in-linkers include m that satisfy the predicate, `p = f1/(P(C)·P(C))` and `q = f3/(P(C)(1−P(C)))`, with f1, f3 the observed fractions of satisfying→satisfying and non-satisfying→satisfying links. The sibling signal is `I_s = s/e`, where s is the number of satisfying visited siblings and `e = n·P(C)` is the random expectation. — [Aggarwal et al. 2001](https://archives.iw3c2.org/www10/cdrom/papers/110/index.html)
- Combined priority: `w_c·log(I_c) + w_u·log(I_u) + w_l·log(I_l) + w_s·log(I_s)`. The weights are set so the components are "roughly balanced" over current candidates, which is a heuristic. A feature is used only if its significance exceeds t = 2 standard deviations (about 95%). `P(C)` is estimated online as `N_c/N_t` (satisfying pages crawled over pages crawled), and all priorities are recomputed every iteration. — [Aggarwal et al. 2001](https://archives.iw3c2.org/www10/cdrom/papers/110/index.html)
- Results: the random baseline harvested about 1%. The intelligent crawler harvested 33% to 41% over about 10,000-page crawls (Table 2; the paper's text and table disagree on the random rates, 0.07 to 0.4%). Reusing knowledge learned on a related predicate raised harvest over the first 5,000 pages from 28% to 42%. — [Aggarwal et al. 2001](https://archives.iw3c2.org/www10/cdrom/papers/110/index.html)
- The survey summarises Aggarwal et al. as learning "on the fly how best to combine relevance signals found in the content of pages linking to p, content of p's 'siblings' … and p's URL, into a single relevance estimate". — [Olston & Najork 2010](https://khoury.northeastern.edu/home/vip/teach/IRcourse/IR_surveys/olston-najork@web-crawling10.pdf)
- **Shark-search (Hersovici et al., WWW7 1998), exact formulas:** `inherited(child) = δ·sim(q,parent)` if the parent is relevant, else `δ·inherited(parent)`. `anchor = sim(q, anchor_text)`. `anchor_context = 1` if anchor > 0, else `sim(q, context)`. `neighbourhood = β·anchor + (1−β)·anchor_context`. `potential(child) = γ·inherited(child) + (1−γ)·neighbourhood(child)`. Best average parameters were δ = 0.5, β = 0.8, γ = 0, depth 3. Sum-of-information over fish-search improved by 1.15× to 3.71× on four informal topics. — [Hersovici et al. 1998](https://archives.iw3c2.org/www7/programme/fullpapers/1849/com1849.html)
- The survey on Shark-search's decay: "This passing along of relevance forces the crawler to traverse irrelevant pages that have relevant ancestors. A decay factor parameter controls how rapidly relevance decays". — [Olston & Najork 2010](https://khoury.northeastern.edu/home/vip/teach/IRcourse/IR_surveys/olston-najork@web-crawling10.pdf)
- **Chakrabarti, van den Berg & Dom (1999):** a classifier judges a page's relevance, and a distiller finds hub pages with a modified HITS. The hard-focus rule expands a page's links only if its most probable class is in the interesting set. The soft-focus rule prioritises children by the summed probability of relevant classes and eliminates nothing a priori. A secondary summary reports an average relevance of about 0.3 to 0.5 sustained over 1000 URLs, while an unfocused crawler drops "practically to 0" almost immediately. — [Chakrabarti et al. 1999 PDF](https://www.math.unipd.it/~dulli/corso04/www1999f.pdf); definitions and numbers via [Novak 2004 summary](https://ailab.ijs.si/Dunja/SiKDD2004/Papers/BlazNovak-FocusedCrawling.pdf) (secondary, not verified against the original)
- **Diligenti et al. (VLDB 2000), context graphs:** each uncrawled page p gets an estimated distance d(p) in links to the nearest relevant page. The crawler fetches all d = 0 pages first, then d = 1, and so on. The relevance threshold controls greediness, and the classifiers are trained on layered back-link context graphs. The survey says the study "showed improved long-term weighted coverage by not being overly greedy". — [Olston & Najork 2010](https://khoury.northeastern.edu/home/vip/teach/IRcourse/IR_surveys/olston-najork@web-crawling10.pdf); [Diligenti et al. 2000 PDF](https://clgiles.ist.psu.edu:443/papers/VLDB-2000-focused-crawling.pdf)
- **Menczer, Pant & Srinivasan (TOIT 2004):** "best performance came from a novel combination of explorative and exploitative bias". An evolutionary crawler (InfoSpiders) "surpasses the performance of the best nonadaptive crawler after sufficiently long crawls". In the survey's words, Menczer's meta-heuristic is to commit to crawling N pages from the current order before reordering, i.e. the BFS-N family, and it showed "there is benefit in not being too greedy". — [Menczer et al. 2004 abstract](https://experts.illinois.edu/en/publications/topical-web-crawlers-evaluating-adaptive-algorithms/); [Olston & Najork 2010](https://khoury.northeastern.edu/home/vip/teach/IRcourse/IR_surveys/olston-najork@web-crawling10.pdf)
- **Pant & Srinivasan:** naive Bayes (Chakrabarti, Diligenti) and SVM (Johnson et al.) classifiers guide topical crawlers, and link-context classifiers score a link from the text around it. — [Pant & Srinivasan TOIS 2005](https://homepage.cs.uiowa.edu/~psriniva/Papers/TOIS05.pdf) (via search snippet)

### Inferences
- **Applying Aggarwal to our features.** Let C = "live public channel". Then `I(C, kind=forward) = 0.85/P(C)`, `I(C, kind=mention-only) = 0.26/P(C)`, `I(C, citers=1) = 0.43/P(C)`, `I(C, citers≥20) = 0.78/P(C)`, `I(C, suffix=bot) ≈ 0.003/P(C)`. Priority is the sum of the log ratios. Example with a base rate P(C) = 0.5: forward + 20+ citers gives log(1.70) + log(1.56), a ratio of about 2.65. Mention-only + 1 citer gives log(0.52) + log(0.86), a ratio of about 0.45. The bot suffix alone is a ratio of about 0.006. That is a 6× spread between the first two and roughly 400× for the bot filter. (My arithmetic on our numbers.)
- **The independence caveat is real here.** Kind and citer count are probably correlated, because forwarded channels tend to have more citers. A product of marginal ratios double-counts shared evidence and overstates the extremes. A logistic regression on our probe log, with features {kind one-hot, log citers, citer-language shares, bot suffix, handle-shape features}, is the same log-odds form but learns weights that account for correlation. It also gives calibrated P(live) directly. Aggarwal's own weights w_c..w_s were a hand balance, so learned weights are an upgrade, not a departure.
- **Relevance is a separate factor from liveness.** A sensible expected value per probe is `EV = P(live | x) · P(relevant | live, x)`. The relevance factor comes from the citing channels' language mix, e.g. the fraction of citers that are Persian/English or that the deployment follows. This mirrors Aggarwal's split between link evidence (who cites it) and content evidence (what the citers contain).
- **Shark-search's inherited score fits "Persian channels reached via Russian citers".** A handle cited by a relevant channel inherits δ·relevance(citer). A handle cited only by irrelevant citers inherits δ² of the grandparent's relevance, so exploration decays geometrically instead of being cut to zero. δ = 0.5 is the paper's tuned value on the web, which is a heuristic for this setting.
- Soft focus fits better than hard focus. A hard rule ("only probe handles with a Persian citer") would never find a Persian channel whose only citer is Russian.

### Gaps
- I could not get exact harvest-rate numbers for Chakrabarti 1999, Diligenti 2000 or Menczer 2004 from primary text. An Academia.edu claim of "50-60% improvement" for Diligenti has no verifiable provenance and is excluded.
- The exact significance-measure formulas in Aggarwal et al. exist only as images in the HTML version.

## 4. Learning-based prioritisation, bandits and exploration (Gouriten et al. 2014; Meusel et al. 2014; Thompson/UCB)

### Takeaway
Gouriten et al. found three things. Greedy-by-estimate beats randomised exploration once crawls run past a few thousand steps. Re-scoring the frontier after every fetch matters a lot: batching 2 or more items degraded the score by 8 to 36%. A bandit choosing among cheap estimators (in-link sums, an online linear regression) was the most stable estimator across crawl stages. Anthelion (Meusel et al.) pairs an online classifier with a bandit over domains for exploration.

### Cited Findings
- Finding the optimal crawl sequence is NP-hard even offline, by reduction to length-constrained maximum-sum subtree. "Even a crawler using a greedy strategy with an oracle estimator … cannot be optimal in the general case". — [Gouriten, Maniu & Senellart, HT 2014](https://www.cl.cam.ac.uk/~ey204/teaching/ACS/R212_2015_2016/papers/gouriten_HT_2014.pdf)
- Greedy vs "altered greedy": with probability q take the argmax, and with probability 1−q take a random frontier node whose estimate is at least ζ·max. Over q ∈ {0.2…1.0} and ζ ∈ {0…0.8}, "for any number of steps greater or equal to a few thousands … all the randomized strategies fall behind greedy". The authors attribute this to "rich people have rich friends". Below about 1,000 steps, altered greedy sometimes won, because "some rich parts can 'hide' behind poor nodes". — [Gouriten et al. 2014](https://www.cl.cam.ac.uk/~ey204/teaching/ACS/R212_2015_2016/papers/gouriten_HT_2014.pdf)
- Batch disadvantage: re-scoring the frontier only every b fetches degrades the score even with an oracle estimator. Table 2 reports degradations from 8.3% to 35.9% as batch size grows, and the paper says "even for a batch size of 2, the performance degradation is notable". (The table's batch-size labels were lost in text extraction, so treat the per-size mapping as unverified.) — [Gouriten et al. 2014](https://www.cl.cam.ac.uk/~ey204/teaching/ACS/R212_2015_2016/papers/gouriten_HT_2014.pdf)
- First-level estimators for a frontier node v with crawled parents P(v): `fl_deg = |P(v)|`; `fl_n = Σ_{u∈P(v)} β(u)` (sum of parent scores); `fl_e = Σ α(u,v)` (sum of edge scores); `fl_ne = Σ β(u)·α(u,v)`. Second-level variants sum over the parents' other children. Log smoothing `f(x) = log(1+x)` raised Pearson correlation with true score sharply, e.g. fl_deg 0.063 → 0.269 and fl_ne 0.090 → 0.412. — [Gouriten et al. 2014](https://www.cl.cam.ac.uk/~ey204/teaching/ACS/R212_2015_2016/papers/gouriten_HT_2014.pdf)
- `lr_fl` is an incremental least-squares linear regression over the fl_ features (recursive least squares via dlib), retrained before each frontier estimation on the crawled graph. — [Gouriten et al. 2014](https://www.cl.cam.ac.uk/~ey204/teaching/ACS/R212_2015_2016/papers/gouriten_HT_2014.pdf)
- Bandit formulation: the arms are the estimators, and the reward is the true weight of the top node the chosen estimator returned. The variants are ε-greedy, ε-first, and ε-first with variable reset, where every r steps averages reset and `r = h(p)` grows with p, the number of consecutive times the same estimator won. The best setting was mab_var-0.2-200, i.e. ε = 0.2, `h(p) = 200·(p+1)`. (Note: the paper's ε-greedy text says the *best* arm is used with probability ε, the reverse of the usual convention.) — [Gouriten et al. 2014](https://www.cl.cam.ac.uk/~ey204/teaching/ACS/R212_2015_2016/papers/gouriten_HT_2014.pdf)
- Global score normalised to oracle-greedy, averaged over 5 graphs (Wikipedia, Twitter), at 100 / 1k / 10k / 100k steps (my de-interleaving of the extracted table, consistent with the authors' prose): bfs 0.15/0.13/0.13/0.21; opic 0.28/0.18/0.21/0.29; n 0.36/0.28/0.36/0.47; e 0.59/0.56/0.46/0.38; ne 0.58/0.57/0.47/0.38; lr_fl 0.33/0.38/0.47/0.50; mab_var 0.43/0.41/0.46/0.46. The prose: "lr_fl is not great at the beginning but performs well in the long run. mab_var does stably well all along the crawl." — [Gouriten et al. 2014](https://www.cl.cam.ac.uk/~ey204/teaching/ACS/R212_2015_2016/papers/gouriten_HT_2014.pdf)
- Recommended design: greedy, batch size 1, first-level estimators, an incremental linear regression over them, and a bandit choosing among the estimators. Estimation cost was 0.1 to 4 ms per step for the first-level estimators, against seconds for Navigational Rank. — [Gouriten et al. 2014](https://www.cl.cam.ac.uk/~ey204/teaching/ACS/R212_2015_2016/papers/gouriten_HT_2014.pdf)
- **Anthelion (Meusel, Mika & Blanco, CIKM 2014)** combines an online classifier, which predicts whether an unfetched page holds structured data from its context and learns from fetched results, with bandit-based explore/exploit selection. The authors report that it "significantly outperform[s]" state-of-the-art focused crawlers on relevant/non-relevant ratio within a budget. — [CIKM 2014 record](https://ir.webis.de/anthology/2014.cikm_conference-2014.104)
- Per the Nutch concept doc, Anthelion's bandit chooses which *domain* to visit next and the classifier scores URLs within that domain. Both update online. It was contributed to Apache Nutch (NUTCH-2202). — [Apache Nutch wiki: Anthelion](https://cwiki.apache.org/confluence/spaces/NUTCH/pages/115512015/Anthelion)

### Inferences
- **The batch finding needs care for us.** Gouriten's degradation comes from rich nodes revealing rich neighbours sooner. A Telegram probe that discovers a live channel does not reveal its references until it is synced. So the feedback loop is slower than in their model, and re-scoring per probe matters less. Re-scoring after each sync or harvest batch is likely enough. Re-scoring is cheap if priority is a logistic score over a few aggregated features.
- **Exploration.** Gouriten says pure greedy wins at scale *when the estimator is good and the graph is homophilous*. Our concern is different: estimator bias against a minority region (Persian behind Russian citers). A per-arm bandit fits that concern better than node-level randomisation. Arms could be feature buckets, e.g. (kind × citer-count bucket × citer-language) cells. Use Thompson sampling on each cell's Beta(successes+1, failures+1) posterior of yield, or reserve a fixed small exploration slice for under-sampled cells. Thompson sampling and UCB are standard, but **no crawling paper I read evaluates them on this problem**, so this is design inference.
- A Beta posterior per bucket is also the simplest way to get the calibrated P(live | bucket) table the user already measured. Updating it from every probe outcome turns the static 85% / 81% / 59% / 26% table into an online estimate.

### Gaps
- I did not obtain Anthelion's quantitative results (full PDF link redirects).
- I found no paper on Thompson-sampling or UCB crawl ordering with reported numbers. Reinforcement-learning focused crawlers (Rennie & McCallum 1999; recent RL crawlers) were not reached within the budget.

## 5. Budget split between discovery (new) and refresh (known)

### Takeaway
No consensus optimum exists. The survey states "There is no consensus about the best way to balance the two." The two published unifications are WebFountain, which treats uncrawled pages as freshness 0, and OPIC, which steers cash through the virtual page. Dasgupta et al. quantify that most new content can be found cheaply by recrawling a small, well-chosen set of known sources, and that the last portion is expensive.

### Cited Findings
- An incremental crawler's two actions are (1) download a new page, which may improve coverage and supply new links, and (2) re-download an old page, which may improve freshness and also supply new links. "There is no consensus about the best way to balance the two. Some contend that coverage and freshness are like apples and oranges, and balancing the two objectives should be left as a business decision". — [Olston & Najork 2010](https://khoury.northeastern.edu/home/vip/teach/IRcourse/IR_surveys/olston-najork@web-crawling10.pdf)
- WebFountain (Edwards et al.) "folds in coverage by treating uncrawled pages as having a freshness value of zero". It buckets pages by change rate and solves a non-linear program over buckets for revisit rates. OPIC "focuses on ensuring coverage of important pages, and in the process periodically revisits old important pages". — [Olston & Najork 2010](https://khoury.northeastern.edu/home/vip/teach/IRcourse/IR_surveys/olston-najork@web-crawling10.pdf)
- OPIC's explicit knob, from the paper: "when we want to do more discovery … (i) we take some cash from the virtual page and distribute it to pages that were not read yet (ii) we increase the importance of 'small' edges pointing to the virtual page … To refresh more pages, we do the opposite." The crawl rate is a single budget shared by discovery and refresh. — [Abiteboul et al. 2003](https://cs.brynmawr.edu/Courses/cs380/fall2006/online_page_importance.pdf)
- Dasgupta et al. (WWW 2007): with perfect foreknowledge, 90% of new content is discoverable at under 3% overhead and 100% at 9% overhead. For realistic algorithms, a quarter of new content is not efficiently discoverable, and 80% of a week's new content costs 160% overhead with full monthly recrawls. Overhead is fetches per new page discovered. A conference-archive abstract gives *different* figures (50% at <3%, 100% at 28%, 26% only via recrawling a constant fraction, 80% at 1.3× cost). I could not confirm which version is final. — [Dasgupta et al. 2007 PDF](https://www.www2007.cpsc.ucalgary.ca/papers/paper592.pdf); conflicting abstract at [archives.iw3c2.org](https://archives.iw3c2.org/www2007/paper592.php.html)
- Survey summary of Dasgupta and Ntoulas: new pages appear at 8% per week and new links at 25% per week. "It is possible to discover 90% of new pages by monitoring links spawned from a small, well-chosen set of old pages (for most sites, five or fewer pages suffice …) However, discovering the remaining 10% requires substantially more effort." Dasgupta used a set-cover formulation to choose those old pages. — [Olston & Najork 2010](https://khoury.northeastern.edu/home/vip/teach/IRcourse/IR_surveys/olston-najork@web-crawling10.pdf)
- The survey's open problems list "holistic crawl ordering": "little work on how to integrate the disparate approaches into a unified strategy". — [Olston & Najork 2010](https://khoury.northeastern.edu/home/vip/teach/IRcourse/IR_surveys/olston-najork@web-crawling10.pdf)

### Inferences
- For us, "refresh" (syncing known channels) is also the *discovery source*, because new handles come from references in fetched posts. Dasgupta's set-cover result says to refresh the channels that historically yield the most new live, relevant handles per fetch more often. Discovery value is then part of each known channel's refresh priority, not a separate pool.
- A defensible split is one common currency rather than a fixed ratio: expected value per probe. For a new handle that is `P(live)·P(relevant|live)`. For a refresh it is the expected new posts plus the expected new-handle yield. Then take greedily from the merged queue with a floor for each side. With no shared value model, a fixed share (e.g. X% discovery) that is tuned by measuring the marginal yield of the last discovery probe against the last refresh probe is the boring, defensible start. This is my inference; no source gives a number for this split.

### Gaps
- No source I found reports an empirically optimal discovery/refresh percentage. Kolobov et al.'s later work on joint discovery/refresh scheduling (e.g. "Staying up to date with online content changes using reinforcement learning", NeurIPS 2019, and related Bing work) was not reached within the budget.

## 6. Cheap pre-fetch prediction of dead/invalid items (URL-only classifiers)

### Takeaway
URL-only classifiers approach full-content classifiers at a tiny fraction of the cost, and character n-grams beat whole tokens. For handles, the "bot" suffix is an extreme single feature. Handle-string n-grams plus reference metadata are the analogue of these URL classifiers.

### Cited Findings
- Kan & Nguyen Thi (CIKM 2005) segment URLs into chunks with component, sequential and orthographic features and feed them to a maximum-entropy model. The approach is far faster because pages need not be fetched, and it "can approach state-of-the-art full-text and link-based methods" in some settings. The tech report says it can predict PageRank-based prestige with an average error under 1 point on a 10-point scale. — [Kan & Nguyen Thi 2005 PDF](https://www.comp.nus.edu.sg/~kanmy/papers/cp689-kan.pdf) (via search summary)
- Baykan, Henzinger, Marian & Weber (WWW 2009) do purely URL-based topic classification on about 1.5M ODP URLs. Letter n-grams (4- to 8-grams, "all-grams") beat tokens. On the university-page task, macro-F was 62.9 against a prior best of 52.5. The TWEB 2011 version over about 3M URLs reports F 80 to 85, typical precision about 86, and precision above 90 at 30 to 40% recall. — [Baykan et al. WWW 2009 PDF](https://infoscience.epfl.ch/record/136823/files/Topic_www_09.pdf?version=1); [TWEB 2011](https://eprints.cs.univie.ac.at/126) (via search summary)
- Pandey & Olston estimate frontier pages' relevance "from cues found in URLs and referring anchortext, as first proposed in the context of scoped crawling". — [Olston & Najork 2010](https://khoury.northeastern.edu/home/vip/teach/IRcourse/IR_surveys/olston-najork@web-crawling10.pdf)

### Inferences
- The URL-classifier result transfers as "a handle-string model is cheap and worth having". Useful handle features: suffix "bot" (already 99.7% dead), character n-grams, digits and underscores, length, script hints (transliterated Persian vs Russian), and whether the handle appeared as a t.me link (a URL a person typed) or only as an @mention. These go into the same logistic model as Section 3's features.
- Treat "bot" as a hard skip, or a near-zero prior with occasional exploration, rather than a soft feature. At 0.3% live rate, a probe costs about 330 probes per live hit.

### Gaps
- I did not verify the Kan and Baykan numbers against the primary PDFs, only through search summaries.

## 7. Fairness across seeds/sources so one subgraph doesn't dominate

### Takeaway
The literature supports per-source caps and round-robin as both politeness and quality devices. Per-domain limits improved relevance metrics (Fetterly et al.). Larger-sites-first drains per-site queues so a few large sites don't monopolise the end of the crawl. Anthelion schedules at the domain level with a bandit. Counting only cross-site links blunts single-source spam.

### Cited Findings
- Per Fetterly et al., "imposing per-domain page limits boosts effectiveness". — [Olston & Najork 2010](https://khoury.northeastern.edu/home/vip/teach/IRcourse/IR_surveys/olston-najork@web-crawling10.pdf)
- Larger-sites-first exists "to avoid having at the end only a small number of large Web sites". In a real .gr crawl it was faster than breadth-first in both documents and PageRank because it "saves" small sites for later. Its authors warn it needs anti-spam checks so sites cannot inflate their page counts. — [Baeza-Yates et al. 2005](https://archives.iw3c2.org/www2005/proceedings/docs/p864.pdf)
- Counting only external links (Najork & Wiener) and biasing PageRank jumps to a trusted set are standard spam defences for link-based ordering. — [Olston & Najork 2010](https://khoury.northeastern.edu/home/vip/teach/IRcourse/IR_surveys/olston-najork@web-crawling10.pdf)
- Anthelion picks a domain with a bandit, then ranks URLs inside it with the classifier. — [Apache Nutch wiki: Anthelion](https://cwiki.apache.org/confluence/spaces/NUTCH/pages/115512015/Anthelion)
- Backlink-count and partial PageRank "get stuck in pages that are locally optimal, and then fail to discover other pages", performing below random. — [Baeza-Yates et al. 2005](https://archives.iw3c2.org/www2005/proceedings/docs/p864.pdf)

### Inferences
- In our setting the "source" is the citing channel, or a cluster of citers such as the Russian-language block. Two cheap devices follow. First, count distinct citers, never raw references, so one prolific channel cannot push its targets up. Second, cap the probes attributable to any single citer per day, or round-robin over citer language groups with weights. The second keeps the Russian 64% of the frontier from consuming most of the budget even when its P(live) is high.
- Combining Sections 3, 4 and 7: score = calibrated P(live) × P(relevant|live). Schedule greedily within language/source buckets, and allocate budget across buckets by bandit (Thompson on per-bucket relevant-yield). That is Anthelion's domain-bandit + classifier structure carried over to Telegram handles. This is my design inference, not a published system.

### Gaps
- I found no study that quantifies fairness caps for social-graph or messaging-platform crawls specifically. The supporting evidence is all from web crawling.
