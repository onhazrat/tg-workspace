# Recrawl / refresh scheduling of known pages under a fixed fetch budget

Notation used throughout these notes (papers use different letters, mapped here):
- `λ_i` (Cho) = `Δ_i` (Azar, Kolobov, Busa-Fekete) = change rate of item i (changes per unit time, Poisson).
- `f_i` (Cho) = `ρ_i` (Azar, Kolobov) = refresh rate of item i.
- `w_i` = `μ_i` (Azar, Kolobov) = importance weight (request rate, PageRank, clicks, any positive stationary weight).
- `τ_i` = time elapsed since item i was last fetched.
- `R` = total fetch budget (fetches per unit time). `μ` (Cho) or `λ` (Azar/Kolobov) = the Lagrange multiplier.

Source-access note: I read full text (via pdftotext) of Cho & Garcia-Molina's Stanford 1999 tech report "Synchronizing a database to improve freshness" (the preprint of the SIGMOD 2000 paper whose journal extension is TODS 2003), Azar et al. PNAS 2018, Kolobov et al. SIGIR 2019, Kolobov et al. NeurIPS 2019, Avrachenkov et al. 2020, Busa-Fekete et al. (Google) 2025, Lefortier et al. (Yandex) CIKM 2013, Avrachenkov & Borkar 2015, Olston & Pandey WWW 2008, Grimes & O'Brien WWW 2008 poster, the Nutch AdaptiveFetchSchedule source, and the 2018 Google data science blog post. I could not retrieve the TODS 2003 or TOIT 2003 PDFs themselves (Cho's UCLA host refused connections), so TODS/TOIT section numbers below are quoted as cited by other papers.

## 1. Cho & Garcia-Molina: freshness, age, and the optimal allocation (uniform beats proportional)

### Takeaway
Under Poisson changes with known rates and equal weights, the freshness-optimal refresh frequency is a non-monotone function of change rate: it rises, peaks, then falls to exactly zero for items that change faster than `1/μ`, and uniform allocation provably beats proportional allocation for any rate distribution. The optimum is the Lagrange condition `∂F̄(λ_i, f_i)/∂f_i = μ` (no closed form for `f`), and the curve has the same shape for every `μ` up to scaling. Fixed-interval (deterministic) revisits beat randomised ones.

### Cited Findings
- Freshness of element `e_i` at time t is `F(e_i; t) = 1` if up to date, else 0; database freshness `F(S;t) = (1/N) Σ F(e_i;t)`. Age is `A(e_i;t) = 0` if up to date, else `t − (modification time of e_i)`. Both are averaged over time: `F̄(e_i) = lim_{t→∞} (1/t) ∫_0^t F(e_i;t) dt` (Definitions 1-3, Section 2.1). [Cho & Garcia-Molina 1999/SIGMOD 2000](https://www.kth.se/social/files/554f1d5bf27654514fd00393/synchronizing-a-database-to-improve-freshness.pdf)
- Under a Poisson change process with rate λ, after a sync at t=0: `E[F(e_i;t)] = e^{−λt}` and `E[A(e_i;t)] = t(1 − (1 − e^{−λt})/(λt))` (Section 2.2). [Cho & Garcia-Molina](https://www.kth.se/social/files/554f1d5bf27654514fd00393/synchronizing-a-database-to-improve-freshness.pdf)
- Time-averaged freshness, with `r = λ/f` (Table 2, Section 4): fixed-order (fixed interval) `F̄ = (1 − e^{−r})/r`; random-order `F̄ = (1/r)(1 − ((1 − e^{−r})/r)^2)`; purely random `F̄ = 1/(1 + r)`. Fixed-order age is `Ā = I(1/2 − 1/r + (1 − e^{−r})/r^2)`; purely random age is `Ā = I·r/(1 + r)`. [Cho & Garcia-Molina](https://www.kth.se/social/files/554f1d5bf27654514fd00393/synchronizing-a-database-to-improve-freshness.pdf)
- At r = 1 (sync as often as it changes) fixed-order freshness is `(e − 1)/e ≈ 0.63`, 30% higher than purely random; fixed-order age is one quarter of random-order age. For freshness ≥ 0.8 with fixed order you need `r < 0.46`, i.e. sync about 2x as often as the item changes (Section 4). [Cho & Garcia-Molina](https://www.kth.se/social/files/554f1d5bf27654514fd00393/synchronizing-a-database-to-improve-freshness.pdf)
- Uniform vs proportional (Section 5.1, Table 3, gamma-distributed rates with `δ² = variance/mean²`): the uniform policy is better than proportional for every r and δ, the advantage grows with δ, and Appendix D proves uniform beats proportional "under any distribution". [Cho & Garcia-Molina](https://www.kth.se/social/files/554f1d5bf27654514fd00393/synchronizing-a-database-to-improve-freshness.pdf)
- Two-element intuition (Section 5.2, Table 4): with λ1 = 9/day and λ2 = 1/day and only 1 sync/day, syncing the slow element yields expected benefit 1/4 day versus 1/36 day for the fast one; "when it is not possible to keep up with everything, it is better to focus on what we can track." The optimum sits between uniform and proportional when the budget is large. [Cho & Garcia-Molina](https://www.kth.se/social/files/554f1d5bf27654514fd00393/synchronizing-a-database-to-improve-freshness.pdf)
- Optimal allocation (Problem 1, Section 5.3; solution in Appendix E): maximise `(1/N) Σ F̄(λ_i, f_i)` subject to `(1/N) Σ f_i = f`, `f_i ≥ 0`. Solution: `∂F̄(λ_i, f_i)/∂f_i = μ` for all i, plus the budget equation; when that equation has no solution with `f_i ≥ 0`, set `f_i = 0`. For fixed order, `∂F̄/∂f = −(1/f)e^{−λ/f} + (1/λ)(1 − e^{−λ/f})`, which has no closed-form solution for f. [Cho & Garcia-Molina](https://www.kth.se/social/files/554f1d5bf27654514fd00393/synchronizing-a-database-to-improve-freshness.pdf)
- Shape of f(λ) (Theorem 5, Appendix E.1): if `(λ, f)` solves `∂F̄/∂f = μ`, then `(μλ, μf)` solves it for `μ = 1`; the curve is a scaled copy for every μ. For μ = 1 the curve peaks at about (λ = 0.53, f = 0.3) and reaches f = 0 at λ = 1; for μ = 2 the peak is at (0.53/2, 0.3/2) and the cutoff at λ = 1/2. Rule: "To improve freshness, we should penalize the elements that change too often." [Cho & Garcia-Molina](https://www.kth.se/social/files/554f1d5bf27654514fd00393/synchronizing-a-database-to-improve-freshness.pdf)
- Worked example (Example 4, Table 5): rates 1..5/day, budget 5 syncs/day; freshness-optimal frequencies are 1.15, 1.36, 1.35, 1.14, 0.00 (the fastest item gets nothing). Age-optimal frequencies are 0.84, 0.97, 1.03, 1.07, 1.09, monotone increasing but close to uniform. [Cho & Garcia-Molina](https://www.kth.se/social/files/554f1d5bf27654514fd00393/synchronizing-a-database-to-improve-freshness.pdf)
- Empirical result (Section 6, 720,000 pages from 270 sites crawled daily Feb to Jun 1999; Table 6, 100M pages synced monthly): freshness 0.12 proportional, 0.57 uniform, 0.62 optimal; age 400 days proportional, 5.6 days uniform, 4.3 days optimal. They also report the Poisson model fits observed change intervals (Figure 11). [Cho & Garcia-Molina](https://www.kth.se/social/files/554f1d5bf27654514fd00393/synchronizing-a-database-to-improve-freshness.pdf)
- The weighted (non-uniform importance) case was only touched on in Cho & Garcia-Molina's TODS 2003 Section 6 with two popularity weights, and solved numerically for small n; Azar et al. say it "appears intractable in general" before their result. [Azar et al., PNAS 2018](https://www.erichorvitz.com/Crawl_1801519115.full.pdf)
- Azar et al. restate the TODS deterministic-schedule objective with weights as `F(ρ) = Σ_i μ_i (1 − exp(−Δ_i/ρ_i))/(Δ_i/ρ_i)` (Eq. 6, citing TODS Section 4). [Azar et al.](https://www.erichorvitz.com/Crawl_1801519115.full.pdf)
- Castillo's thesis, as quoted by Azar et al., summarises: optimal freshness ignores pages that change too often; optimal age uses access frequencies that increase monotonically and sublinearly with change rate; explicit formulas are not attainable in general. [Azar et al.](https://www.erichorvitz.com/Crawl_1801519115.full.pdf)

### Inferences
- With importance weights, the Cho Lagrange condition becomes `w_i · ∂F̄(λ_i, f_i)/∂f_i = μ`. Since `∂F̄/∂f → 1/λ` as `f → 0+`, item i gets a non-zero frequency only if `w_i/λ_i > μ`. That is the same "value ratio" threshold Azar et al. derive for the Poisson-crawl version (Section 3 below), so the cutoff rule is robust to the schedule model.
- Scale for this deployment: 500k channels at ~30k fetches/day is a mean of 0.06 fetches/channel/day (one every ~17 days). Many channels post several times per day, so most of the corpus is in the `r = λ/f ≫ 1` regime where Cho says binary freshness is unattainable. The binary-freshness optimum would zero out most active channels; this is a reason to prefer the harmonic or count-based objectives in Section 3.
- The fixed-order result argues for deterministic `next_due_at` scheduling rather than random sampling of channels.

### Gaps
- I could not read the TODS 2003 paper itself; its exact section numbering for the weighted-freshness extension and any age closed form beyond the 1999 report is unverified.

## 2. Change-rate estimation (Cho & Garcia-Molina TOIT 2003 and successors), including count observations

### Takeaway
When a fetch only reveals "changed or not", the naive `X/T` estimator is biased low (it counts at most one change per interval); Cho & Garcia-Molina's bias-corrected estimator `λ̂ = −log((n − X + 0.5)/(n + 0.5))/I` fixes regular intervals, and an MLE root-finding equation handles irregular intervals. Telegram preview pages give the exact count of new posts per fetch (the post-id delta), which removes the censoring problem: the Poisson-count MLE is simply total new posts divided by total observed time.

### Cited Findings
- Naive estimator: changes observed divided by time observed. It is "significantly asymptotically biased" when crawl interval is comparable to change interval, because multiple changes in one interval register as one. Cho & Garcia-Molina TOIT Section 4.2 subtract the expected bias and add a 0.5 correction to remove singularities, giving (in per-interval units, n = number of intervals, X = intervals with a detected change) `Δ̂* = −1/log((T − X + 0.5)/(T + 0.5))` as a mean change interval, i.e. rate `λ̂ = −log((n − X + 0.5)/(n + 0.5))/I`. It "is demonstrated to have significantly better bias, even for small (N < 50) observations, especially when C/Δ is in the neighborhood of 0.5−2.0" (C = crawl interval). [Grimes & O'Brien, WWW 2008 poster](https://archives.iw3c2.org/www2008/papers/pdf/p1149-grimes.pdf)
- The same poster warns that if samples are significantly non-Poisson the asymptotic results do not apply, and found a spike of pages changing at exactly 24-hour intervals (daily automated updates) among 29,000 fast-changing URLs crawled hourly. [Grimes & O'Brien](https://archives.iw3c2.org/www2008/papers/pdf/p1149-grimes.pdf)
- Irregular-interval MLE (Cho & Garcia-Molina TOIT Appendix C): with intervals `a_j = t_j − t_{j−1}` and `z_j = 1` if any change was seen, `λ̂` solves `Σ_{j: z_j=1} a_j/(e^{a_j λ} − 1) = Σ_{j: z_j=0} a_j` (Eq. 10). The left side decreases monotonically in λ, so it is solvable numerically, and it is consistent if the intervals do not shrink to 0. [Kolobov et al., NeurIPS 2019](https://papers.nips.cc/paper_files/paper/2019/file/ad13a2a07ca4b7642959dc0c4c740ab6-Paper.pdf); same equation as Eq. 9 in [Avrachenkov, Patil, Thoppe 2020](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2004.02167)
- Singularity smoothing used in LambdaLearnAndCrawl (Algorithm 4, lines 11 and 13): MLE gives `λ̂ = ∞` if every crawl detects a change and the count estimator gives 0 with no observations; they add imaginary observation intervals of length 0.5 to Eq. 10 and an imaginary 0.5 observation to Eq. 11, i.e. `Σ_{z=1} a_j/(e^{a_j λ} − 1) + 0.5/(e^{0.5λ} − 1) − Σ_{z=0} a_j − 0.5 = 0`, and `λ̂ = (U + 0.5)/(S + 0.5)`. [Kolobov et al., NeurIPS 2019](https://papers.nips.cc/paper_files/paper/2019/file/ad13a2a07ca4b7642959dc0c4c740ab6-Paper.pdf)
- Complete change observations (every change is seen): a consistent MLE is the observation rate `λ̂ = (U + 1)/t_U` (Eq. 11, citing Taylor & Karlin). [Kolobov et al., NeurIPS 2019](https://papers.nips.cc/paper_files/paper/2019/file/ad13a2a07ca4b7642959dc0c4c740ab6-Paper.pdf)
- Under Poisson crawling at known rate `p_i`, the naive estimator `z_k = p_i Î_k/k` converges to `p_i Δ_i/(Δ_i + p_i) ≠ Δ_i`, so it is biased and inconsistent. Their online LLN estimator `x_k = p_i Î_k/(k + α_k − Î_k)` (α_k e.g. 1, log k, √k) and SA estimator `y_{k+1} = y_k + η_k[I_{k+1}(y_k + p_i) − y_k]` are consistent, with `E|x_k − Δ_i| = O(max(k^{−1/2}, α_k/k))` and `E|y_k − Δ_i| = O(k^{−γ/2})` for step size `(k+1)^{−γ}`. The MLE lacks a closed form and can be unstable; these online estimators never are. [Avrachenkov et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2004.02167)
- Online learning with only single-bit "changed since last refresh" feedback: Upadhyay et al. give a rate estimator with confidence intervals from time between observations and show an explore-and-commit algorithm attains O(√T) regret. [Upadhyay et al., "Learning to Crawl", AAAI 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/1905.12781)
- Kolobov et al. used the incomplete-history MLE on the Bing dataset because "in spite of almost daily recrawls, we could not guarantee that we observed every page change". [Kolobov et al., SIGIR 2019](https://erichorvitz.com/SIGIR_2019_crawl_under_politeness_constraints.pdf)
- Non-stationarity: rates vary with time, so the history suffix used for estimation should be shorter than the whole history. [Kolobov et al., NeurIPS 2019](https://papers.nips.cc/paper_files/paper/2019/file/ad13a2a07ca4b7642959dc0c4c740ab6-Paper.pdf). Yandex estimates each source's new-link rate from the last T crawls because it "may change during the day or during the week". [Lefortier et al., CIKM 2013](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1307.6080)

### Inferences
- Telegram is the "count observation" case, which none of the web papers needs: each fetch at time `t_j` yields `N_j = latest_post_id(t_j) − latest_post_id(t_{j−1})` new posts. If posts are Poisson with rate λ, `N_j ~ Poisson(λ a_j)`, the log-likelihood is `Σ (N_j log(λ a_j) − λ a_j)`, and the MLE is closed-form: `λ̂ = Σ N_j / Σ a_j`. It is unbiased with variance `λ/Σa_j`, has no censoring bias at any crawl interval, and needs no root-finding. In SQL this is two running sums per channel (`posts_seen`, `seconds_observed`). This is standard Poisson-process statistics derived by me, not taken from a crawl paper.
- Caveat for post-id deltas: deleted posts and service messages consume ids, so `N_j` slightly overcounts "posts that still exist"; for a scheduler this bias is usually harmless, but it should be measured on a sample.
- A conjugate gamma prior gives smoothing and cold start in one formula: posterior mean `λ̂ = (α₀ + Σ N_j)/(β₀ + Σ a_j)`. Kolobov's "+0.5 imaginary observation" is the special case α₀ = β₀ = 0.5. α₀/β₀ can be a per-segment prior (language, subscriber bucket). Inference, not from a fetched source.
- Exponential decay of old evidence (multiply both running sums by `e^{−a_j/H}` at each update, half-life H) implements Kolobov's "shorter history suffix" in O(1) storage per channel.

### Gaps
- Cho & Garcia-Molina TOIT 2003 also has estimators for when last-modified dates are known; I could not access the paper and found no secondary source that states those formulas, so they are not reported.
- Exact bias/variance expressions for the improved estimator (TOIT Section 4.2) are not reproduced here beyond the Grimes & O'Brien summary.

## 3. Microsoft Research / Bing: Azar et al. (PNAS 2018), BinaryLambdaCrawl, PoliteBinaryLambdaCrawl (SIGIR 2019), LambdaCrawl (NeurIPS 2019)

### Takeaway
Azar et al. turned the weighted binary-freshness problem into an exact O(n log n) sort-and-threshold algorithm with closed-form rates `ρ_i = sqrt(μ_i Δ_i/λ) − Δ_i` and a drop rule `μ_i/Δ_i ≤ λ`. Kolobov et al. extended it to per-host politeness (iterative saturation, optimal) and then replaced binary staleness with a harmonic penalty `H(n)` over the number of uncrawled changes, whose optimum `ρ_i = (−Δ_i + sqrt(Δ_i² + 4μ_iΔ_i/λ))/2` never starves a changing source; on 18.5M Bing URLs this beat the binary-optimal policy by 35% on harmonic cost while staying near-optimal on binary cost.

### Cited Findings
- Model: page i has importance `μ_i > 0` (e.g. Poisson request rate) and Poisson change rate `Δ_i`; crawl is a Poisson process with rate `ρ_i`; objective is time-averaged expected weighted binary freshness `lim (1/T) E ∫ Σ μ_i 1{Fresh(i,t)} dt`. [Kolobov et al., SIGIR 2019, Eq. 1](https://erichorvitz.com/SIGIR_2019_crawl_under_politeness_constraints.pdf)
- Problem 2 (continuous-time randomised policy): maximise `F₂(ρ) = Σ μ_i ρ_i/(ρ_i + Δ_i)` subject to `Σ ρ_i = R`, `ρ_i ≥ 0` (Eq. 5). Discrete-time analogue: maximise `Σ μ_i p_i/(p_i + Δ_i − p_iΔ_i)` with `Σ p_i = 1` (Eq. 3). [Azar et al., PNAS 2018](https://www.erichorvitz.com/Crawl_1801519115.full.pdf)
- Solution (Eq. 11): `ρ_i = sqrt(μ_iΔ_i/λ) − Δ_i` with `sqrt(λ) = Σ sqrt(μ_iΔ_i)/(R + Σ Δ_i)` over the active set; `ρ_i ≤ 0` iff `μ_i/Δ_i ≤ λ`. Algorithm 2: sort by `μ_i/Δ_i` ascending; `r ← Σ sqrt(μ_iΔ_i)`, `s ← Σ Δ_i`; for each i in order, if `μ_i/Δ_i ≤ r²/(R + s)^2` then `ρ_i ← 0`, `r ← r − sqrt(μ_iΔ_i)`, `s ← s − Δ_i`, else `ρ_i ← sqrt(μ_iΔ_i)(R + s)/r − Δ_i`. Theorem 1: the optimum is unique and found in O(n log n). [Azar et al.](https://www.erichorvitz.com/Crawl_1801519115.full.pdf); same pseudocode as Algorithm 1 "BinaryLambdaCrawl" in [Kolobov et al., SIGIR 2019](https://erichorvitz.com/SIGIR_2019_crawl_under_politeness_constraints.pdf)
- Derandomisation: a deterministic schedule with the same rates does at least as well because `(1 − e^{−x})/x ≥ 1/(1 + x)`, so `F(ρ) ≥ F₂(ρ)`. Algorithm 3 (EDF derandomisation): keep a credit `δ_k += ρ_k` per slot, among `δ_k > 0` pick the smallest deadline `D_k = ⌈(1 − δ_k)/ρ_k⌉`, then `δ_k −= 1`; it guarantees `|tρ_k − N_{k,t}| ≤ 1` and runs in O(n/ε). In experiments (1,000 synthetic pages, Uniform[0,1] change rates, Zipf or uniform utilities) the deterministic policy attained 99% of the numerically solved optimum. [Azar et al.](https://www.erichorvitz.com/Crawl_1801519115.full.pdf)
- PoliteBinaryLambdaCrawl (Algorithm 2): solve BinaryLambdaCrawl ignoring politeness; for every host whose `Σ_{i∈S_h} ρ_i > R_h`, re-solve that host alone with budget `R_h` as an equality, remove the host, subtract `R_h` from R, recurse. Lemma 3.3 proves any host violated by the unconstrained optimum is saturated in the constrained optimum; Theorem 3.2: optimal in O(n log n + mn). In practice ≤ 6 iterations on 18.5M URLs and 1,688 constrained hosts. [Kolobov et al., SIGIR 2019](https://erichorvitz.com/SIGIR_2019_crawl_under_politeness_constraints.pdf)
- ImportanceCrawl `ρ_i = Rμ_i/Σμ` and ChangeRateCrawl `ρ_i = RΔ_i/ΣΔ` are both optimal when the value ratio `μ_i/Δ_i` is the same constant for all pages (Propositions 1-2), "the first optimality guarantee for importance-proportional crawl scheduling, previously thought to be a mere heuristic". On the full 18.5M set PoliteImportanceCrawl was ~10% worse than PoliteBinaryLambdaCrawl at Cp = 0.2, but 50% worse on the 100k-URL prefix with very non-uniform value ratios. Advantage of the optimal method is largest under tight politeness constraints. [Kolobov et al., SIGIR 2019](https://erichorvitz.com/SIGIR_2019_crawl_under_politeness_constraints.pdf)
- Dataset: 26.6M URLs crawled ~daily for ~14 weeks by Bing; 18.5M changed at least once; change defined by Bing information-extraction templates that filter ads and related-link lists; budget set to `R = 0.2 Σ Δ̂_i` (20% of total change rate). [Kolobov et al., SIGIR 2019](https://erichorvitz.com/SIGIR_2019_crawl_under_politeness_constraints.pdf)
- Harmonic staleness objective (NeurIPS Eq. 1-2): `J^π = lim (1/T) E ∫ Σ_w μ_w C(N_w(t)) dt`, where `N_w(t)` is the number of uncrawled changes and `C(n) = H(n) = Σ_{i=1}^n 1/i`. With binary `C(n) = 1{n>0}` many optimal `ρ*_w` are 0, which "leaves the tracker stale w.r.t. some sources forever"; with harmonic C any policy that ignores a changing source has `J = ∞`. The results hold for any concave `C(n) ≥ 0` with `C(n) → ∞`. [Kolobov et al., NeurIPS 2019](https://papers.nips.cc/paper_files/paper/2019/file/ad13a2a07ca4b7642959dc0c4c740ab6-Paper.pdf)
- Incomplete observations (Prop. 1-2, LambdaCrawl-IncomplObs, Algorithm 1): for Poisson crawling, `J^π = −Σ μ_w ln(ρ_w/(Δ_w + ρ_w))`; optimum `ρ_w = (−Δ_w + sqrt(Δ_w² + 4μ_wΔ_w/λ))/2` with `Σ ρ_w = R`; λ found by bisection in O(log₂((λ_upper − λ_lower)/ε)·|W|). The solution always has λ > 0, so every source gets ρ > 0. [Kolobov et al., NeurIPS 2019](https://papers.nips.cc/paper_files/paper/2019/file/ad13a2a07ca4b7642959dc0c4c740ab6-Paper.pdf)
- Complete observations (change notifications, e.g. sitemaps; Prop. 4-6, LambdaCrawl-ComplObs): crawl on each notification with probability `p_w`; `J^π = −Σ μ_w ln(p_w)`; relaxed optimum `p̂_w = Rμ_w/(Δ_w Σ μ)`; clamp any `p̂_w ≥ 1` to 1, subtract `Δ_w` from R, iterate; optimal, O(|W°|²), close to linear in practice. Mixed observability (LambdaCrawl, Algorithm 3): golden-section search over the bandwidth split between the two classes. [Kolobov et al., NeurIPS 2019](https://papers.nips.cc/paper_files/paper/2019/file/ad13a2a07ca4b7642959dc0c4c740ab6-Paper.pdf)
- LambdaLearnAndCrawl (Algorithm 4): epochs (one day in experiments); each epoch re-solve LambdaCrawl on current estimates, execute, re-estimate with the smoothed MLEs; converges in probability to the optimal policy if rates are stationary (Prop. 8). LambdaCrawlApprox (Prop. 9): if `μ_w/Δ_w = c` for all w, `ρ*_w = μ_w R/Σμ`, no rate learning needed. [Kolobov et al., NeurIPS 2019](https://papers.nips.cc/paper_files/paper/2019/file/ad13a2a07ca4b7642959dc0c4c740ab6-Paper.pdf)
- Results on 18.5M URLs, bandwidth = 20% of URL count: LC beats binary-optimal BLC (harmonic cost infinite) and the non-starving BLC variant by 35% on harmonic cost, while BLC does not beat LC on binary cost. ChangeRateCrawl scored harmonic 2144 / binary 963 and UniformCrawl 1268 / 628, both far worse. Using complete observations for the 4% of URLs that have them gives at least a 2x reduction in harmonic cost on that subset. In 21-day RL simulations from a 1/day initial guess, LLCA converges faster than LLC but to a worse asymptote. [Kolobov et al., NeurIPS 2019](https://papers.nips.cc/paper_files/paper/2019/file/ad13a2a07ca4b7642959dc0c4c740ab6-Paper.pdf)
- Kolobov et al. note the problem can be viewed as a restless multi-armed bandit, but no known restless-bandit class allows arms to incur cost without being pulled, so existing analysis did not apply. [Kolobov et al., NeurIPS 2019](https://papers.nips.cc/paper_files/paper/2019/file/ad13a2a07ca4b7642959dc0c4c740ab6-Paper.pdf)
- Google's 2025 follow-up says sitemap change signals on the Kolobov dataset have precision below 0.2 and recall below 0.5, so the "complete and accurate" observation model is too strong. [Busa-Fekete et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2502.02430v3)

### Inferences
- Telegram fetches are neither of Kolobov's cases: changes are not pushed (so not "complete observations" in their sense), but each fetch reveals the exact count `N_w` of uncollected changes. That makes the harmonic objective directly measurable per fetch (`H(N_w)` is the penalty realised at that fetch), and the rate estimate exact-count (Section 2).
- For a summarizer whose cost is "posts not yet ingested", a linear penalty `C(n) = n` is concave (weakly) and diverges, so Kolobov's framework applies. Under Poisson crawling `E[N_w] = Δ_w/ρ_w`, so cost `Σ μ_wΔ_w/ρ_w` subject to `Σρ = R` gives the square-root rule `ρ_w = R·sqrt(μ_wΔ_w)/Σ sqrt(μΔ)`. This is my derivation, not a result stated in the papers; it sits between ImportanceCrawl and ChangeRateCrawl and never starves.
- The constant-value-ratio result suggests a cheap check: if `w_i/λ̂_i` is roughly constant across the bulk of channels, importance-proportional scheduling is near-optimal; measure it on a sample before building the full solver.

- No "MinBound" algorithm appeared in any of the Kolobov papers I read; I found no source for it.
- The NeurIPS supplement (proofs, binary-cost formulas Eqs. 12-13) was not retrieved.

## 4. Other works: Olston & Pandey, Pandey & Olston, Wolf et al., Edwards et al., Yandex, Whittle index, Upadhyay, Google 2025

### Takeaway
Three independent derivations (Olston & Pandey 2008, Lefortier et al. 2013 at Yandex, and Google's 2018 blog / 2025 paper) arrive at the same threshold rule: refresh item i when `(w_i/λ_i)·g(λ_i τ_i)` reaches a global multiplier, with `g(x) = 1 − (1 + x)e^{−x}`. Yandex's ECHO is the closest published analogue to Telegram channels: periodically recrawl "content sources" that emit new items, with a budget split between source recrawls and fetching what they reveal.

### Cited Findings
- Olston & Pandey: for any monotone per-page divergence curve `D*_P(t)` (t since last refresh), Lagrange multipliers show the policy "refresh exactly those pages that have `U_P(t − t_P) ≥ T`" with `U_P(t) = t·D*_P(t) − ∫_0^t D*_P(x)dx` (Eq. 4) minimises divergence among all policies with the same number of refreshes; T is a utility threshold. [Olston & Pandey, WWW 2008](https://archives.iw3c2.org/www2008/papers/pdf/p437-olstonA4.pdf)
- Specialised to Poisson binary staleness `D*_P(t) = 1 − e^{−λ_P t}`, the utility is `U_P(t) = 1/λ_P − (t + 1/λ_P)e^{−λ_P t}`, and thresholding it "results in identical schedules to those resulting from the optimal policy derived in" Cho & Garcia-Molina TODS 2003 (Appendix). [Olston & Pandey](https://archives.iw3c2.org/www2008/papers/pdf/p437-olstonA4.pdf)
- Olston & Pandey's fragment-staleness metric (Jaccard over shingles) separates churning content (ads) from scrolling content that persists; 67% of non-static fragments showed churn and 33% scroll behaviour; adaptive (dynamic-input) policies "vastly outperform their static counterparts", and their policy beats Cho & Garcia-Molina's on fragment staleness per refresh cost, substantially at moderate cost. [Olston & Pandey](https://archives.iw3c2.org/www2008/papers/pdf/p437-olstonA4.pdf)
- Pandey & Olston's user-centric objective (WWW 2005) depends on user behaviour and is not separable per source; it "can be optimized only via many approximations" and the algorithm is "ultimately heuristic". Wolf et al.'s embarrassment objective (WWW 2002) is implemented via specially constructed importance scores, so separable-objective algorithms can be used for it. [Kolobov et al., NeurIPS 2019, related work](https://papers.nips.cc/paper_files/paper/2019/file/ad13a2a07ca4b7642959dc0c4c740ab6-Paper.pdf)
- Yandex ECHO model: sources S_i emit new links at rate `λ_i`; a new item crawled `Δt` after creation has profit `P_i e^{−μ_i Δt}` (clicks; fitted from logs, e.g. news `25(1 − e^{−1.2·age})` clicks, blogs `23(1 − e^{−0.5·age})`). Recrawling S_i every `I_i` costs `(1 + λ_i I_i)/I_i` fetches per second (the source plus its new pages), so `Σ 1/I_i = N − Σ λ_i`. [Lefortier et al., CIKM 2013](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1307.6080)
- ECHO optimum (Eq. 4): `p_i(1 − (1 + μ_i I_i)e^{−μ_i I_i}) = ω` for all i, with `p_i = P_i/(1 − e^{−μ_i/λ_i})`. Since `g(x) = 1 − (1+x)e^{−x}` rises from 0 to 1, `I_i = g^{−1}(ω/p_i)/μ_i`, sources with `ω > p_i` are never recrawled, and ω is found by binary search (Algorithm 1). [Lefortier et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1307.6080)
- ECHO-greedy (simplification): crawl the source with the highest `λ_i P_i τ_i` (expected profit accumulated since last crawl), then its new pages; "it only requires a priority queue of content sources". Data: ~3K content sources from 100 Russian news sites and 50 blogs, crawled every 10 minutes for 3 weeks, ~415K new pages. The upper bound (crawl everything instantly) is 0.72 profit/s; ECHO-newpages and ECHO-schedule reach it at 0.2 crawls/s while BFS needs 1 crawl/s to reach 99% of it; ECHO-greedy's profit is "also high"; fixed quotas are "far from being optimal". The optimal policy barely recrawls 70% of sources. [Lefortier et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1307.6080)
- Yandex also found 3K content sources cover 80% of ephemeral new content, and 86% of them are at most one hop from a site's main page. [Lefortier et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1307.6080)
- Whittle index for ephemeral sources (Avrachenkov & Borkar 2015): content arrives at source i at Poisson rate `Λ_i` with mean base utility `ξ̄_i` decaying at rate `μ_i`; per-period reward of crawling `u_i = (Λ_i ξ̄_i/μ_i)(1 − e^{−μ_i T})`; state `X_i(t+1) = α_i X_i(t) + u_i` if passive (α_i = e^{−μ_i T}), reward `X_i(t)` and reset to `u_i` if crawled. The problem is Whittle-indexable (Lemma 4) and the index (Theorem 1, simplified) is `γ_i(x) = (η_i((1 − α_i)x − u_i) + x)/C_i` with `η_i(x) = ⌈log_{α_i}((u_i − (1 − α_i)x)/u_i)⌉⁺`; crawl the top M sources by `γ_i(X_i(t))` each period. `C_i` lets sources have different crawl costs. [Avrachenkov & Borkar 2015](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1503.08558)
- Whittle index policies are suboptimal in general but asymptotically optimal and empirically strong for restless bandits. [Avrachenkov & Borkar 2015](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1503.08558)
- Google 2025 (noisy change-indicating signals): optimal policies are threshold policies on an "effective elapsed time" `τ_eff = τ_elap + β_i n_cis` (Lemma 1, Theorem 1); with no signals the per-page crawl value is `V(τ) = (μ̃/Δ)·R¹_exp(Δτ)`, where `R¹_exp(x) = (e^x − 1 − x)/e^x = 1 − (1 + x)e^{−x}`. Discrete policy (Algorithm 1): at each slot crawl `argmax_i V(τ_eff_i(t); E_i)`, which follows from Theorem 1 by setting the Lagrange multiplier to the max crawl value. It is decentralised except for the argmax, keeps total crawl rate constant, adapts to bandwidth changes without recomputation, and gave "a small, albeit significant, improvement" over Azar et al. [Busa-Fekete et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2502.02430v3)
- Google 2025 also cites an estimate that up to 53% of major search engines' crawl traffic is redundant (fetches an unchanged page). [Busa-Fekete et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2502.02430v3)
- Upadhyay et al. (AAAI 2020) learn rates online from single-bit feedback building on Azar et al.'s policy; explore-and-commit with O(√T) regret; simulations "perform close to optimal". [Upadhyay et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1905.12781)

### Inferences
- The Olston & Pandey, Yandex and Google value functions are the same function `g(x)/λ`. A Telegram scheduler with binary "anything new?" semantics can use it directly; one with per-post value and interest decay maps onto ECHO, where `λ_i` is the channel's posting rate and `μ_i` the decay of a post's value to the deployment.
- ECHO's cost model (one source fetch plus `λ_i I_i` item fetches) maps to the ~13-post window: if more than ~13 posts appear between fetches, extra paginated fetches are needed to collect the rest, so the effective cost of a channel visit is `1 + max(0, ⌈(λ_i I_i − 13)/k⌉)` page loads (k posts per page). This argues for not letting fast channels exceed the window, a constraint none of the web papers has.

### Gaps
- I did not retrieve Edwards, McCurley & Tomlin (WWW 2001), Wolf et al. (WWW 2002) or Prokhorenkova et al. (Yandex, popularity-prediction crawling) full texts; only second-hand descriptions are available above (Wolf via Kolobov). No formulas from them are reported.
- Olston & Najork's survey and Castillo's thesis were not read directly.

## 5. Production heuristics (Nutch AdaptiveFetchSchedule) versus optimal policies

### Takeaway
Nutch's adaptive schedule multiplies the interval by 0.8 on change and 1.2 on no change, bounded to [1 minute, 365 days], with no notion of importance or global budget. It drives each page's interval toward its own change interval, which is close to proportional allocation, the policy Cho & Garcia-Molina proved is worse than uniform.

### Cited Findings
- AdaptiveFetchSchedule: on `STATUS_MODIFIED`, `interval *= (1 − DEC_RATE)`; on `STATUS_NOTMODIFIED`, `interval *= (1 + INC_RATE)`; unknown status leaves it unchanged; clamp to [min, max] (host-specific overrides allowed). Javadoc warns that factors above 0.4 "may destabilize" the algorithm. [Nutch source](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/apache/nutch/master/src/java/org/apache/nutch/crawl/AdaptiveFetchSchedule.java)
- Defaults: `db.fetch.schedule.adaptive.inc_rate = 0.2`, `dec_rate = 0.2`, `min_interval = 60 s`, `max_interval = 365 days`, `sync_delta = true`, `sync_delta_rate = 0.2`. SYNC_DELTA: `delta = fetchTime − modifiedTime`; if delta exceeds the interval, the interval becomes delta; next fetch is `refTime + interval` where `refTime = fetchTime − delta·SYNC_DELTA_RATE`. [Nutch source](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/apache/nutch/master/src/java/org/apache/nutch/crawl/AdaptiveFetchSchedule.java)
- Proportional allocation (rate ∝ change rate) gave freshness 0.12 vs 0.62 optimal and age 400 days vs 4.3 days in Cho & Garcia-Molina's web-data projection. [Cho & Garcia-Molina](https://www.kth.se/social/files/554f1d5bf27654514fd00393/synchronizing-a-database-to-improve-freshness.pdf)
- ChangeRateCrawl did poorly on Bing data (harmonic 2144, binary 963) versus UniformCrawl (1268, 628), both omitted from the plot as far worse than LambdaCrawl. [Kolobov et al., NeurIPS 2019](https://papers.nips.cc/paper_files/paper/2019/file/ad13a2a07ca4b7642959dc0c4c740ab6-Paper.pdf)
- Google's crawl team found a large distributed solver was the wrong fit; they deployed a per-page value function with thresholds instead: recrawl when `V(τ) = (w/δ)(1 − (1 + δτ)e^{−δτ})` reaches `λ_host + λ_0`, with each host's pages sorted by V and the top N taken when capacity opens. `λ_host` is maintained per host from recently crawled pages; `λ_0` is estimated centrally from unconstrained hosts, is "fairly stable", and is pushed to workers. They describe the value-function approach as "not greedy" in the starvation sense and list lessons: match the formulation to the system's real decision, prefer an understandable heuristic, and remember a batch solver's runtime bounds how fast it reacts. [Richoux, Unofficial Google Data Science Blog, 2018](https://www.unofficialgoogledatascience.com/2018/07/by-bill-richoux-critical-decisions-are.html)

### Inferences
- Nutch-style multiplicative backoff converges each interval toward roughly the item's own change interval (it shortens after changes and lengthens after misses until both happen at similar rates). That makes it a proportional policy in disguise, plus it ignores `w_i` and the global budget. With a fixed budget much smaller than total change volume, it will spend most fetches on the fastest channels.
- Exponential backoff is still useful as a cold-start or fallback for channels with no rate estimate, but the score in Section 6 is cheaper and better grounded.

### Gaps
- I found no published head-to-head measurement of Nutch's AdaptiveFetchSchedule against Cho/Azar-style optimal policies.

## 6. A single sortable priority column combining importance and change rate

### Takeaway
The best-supported single column is the Lagrangian crawl value `score_i = (w_i/λ_i)·(1 − (1 + λ_i τ_i)·e^{−λ_i τ_i})`, re-evaluated as τ grows; fetch the top-K by score each tick, or fetch when score exceeds a multiplier tuned to hit the budget. It is optimal for weighted binary freshness (Olston & Pandey's equivalence proof, Google 2025 Theorem 1) and is what Google's production crawl uses. Simpler alternatives with published support are Yandex's `λ_i·P_i·τ_i` and Kolobov's closed-form rates turned into `next_due_at = last_fetch + 1/ρ_i`.

### Cited Findings
- Binary freshness crawl value: `V(τ) = (μ/Δ)(1 − (1 + Δτ)e^{−Δτ})`; crawl the argmax each slot (Algorithm 1); the global multiplier equals the max crawl value, so no global solve is needed. [Busa-Fekete et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2502.02430v3); same function in [Richoux 2018](https://www.unofficialgoogledatascience.com/2018/07/by-bill-richoux-critical-decisions-are.html) and, without weight, `U_P(t) = 1/λ − (t + 1/λ)e^{−λt}` in [Olston & Pandey 2008](https://archives.iw3c2.org/www2008/papers/pdf/p437-olstonA4.pdf)
- Google notes V is increasing, starts at 0 and is bounded (by `w/δ`), "so some pages may never be recrawled". [Richoux 2018](https://www.unofficialgoogledatascience.com/2018/07/by-bill-richoux-critical-decisions-are.html)
- Yandex ECHO-greedy priority `λ_i P_i τ_i` (expected value of new items accumulated since last fetch) needs only a priority queue and performed close to the optimal schedule. [Lefortier et al. 2013](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1307.6080)
- Yandex's optimal schedule uses the same `g`: `p_i·g(μ_i I_i) = ω`, where μ is the interest-decay rate rather than the change rate. [Lefortier et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1307.6080)
- Static-rate alternative: compute `ρ_i` once per epoch (Azar's `sqrt(μ_iΔ_i/λ) − Δ_i` or Kolobov's harmonic `(−Δ_i + sqrt(Δ_i² + 4μ_iΔ_i/λ))/2`) and schedule deterministically (EDF credits). [Azar et al.](https://www.erichorvitz.com/Crawl_1801519115.full.pdf); [Kolobov et al., NeurIPS 2019](https://papers.nips.cc/paper_files/paper/2019/file/ad13a2a07ca4b7642959dc0c4c740ab6-Paper.pdf)
- Whittle index alternative for decaying-value sources: `γ_i(x) = (η_i((1 − α_i)x − u_i) + x)/C_i` on the accumulated-utility state x. [Avrachenkov & Borkar 2015](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1503.08558)

### Inferences
- SQL form, recomputed in the planning query (no stored score needed because τ changes every second):
  `score = w * (1 - (1 + lam*tau) * exp(-lam*tau)) / lam`, with `tau = extract(epoch from now() - last_fetched_at)/86400` and `lam = (alpha0 + posts_seen)/(beta0 + days_observed)`. For `lam*tau < ~1e-4` use the series `w*lam*tau^2/2` to avoid cancellation. Then `ORDER BY score DESC LIMIT k`.
- Behaviour of this score: for slow channels (`λτ ≪ 1`) score ≈ `w·λ·τ²/2`, quadratic in elapsed time, so quiet channels are revisited eventually; for fast channels it saturates at `w/λ`, so a channel posting 100x/day with the same weight is worth 1/100 of one posting once a day once both are stale. That is Cho's "penalize the elements that change too often" in one column.
- If the deployment's real objective is "posts not yet ingested" rather than "is the copy stale", the score should not saturate. With linear penalty `C(n) = n`, the expected backlog is `λτ`, and the fetch value is `w_i·λ_i·τ_i`, which is exactly Yandex's ECHO-greedy priority. A blend that also respects the 13-post window is `w_i·min(λ_i τ_i, 13)`, which stops growing once a fetch can no longer collect everything. These are my derivations from the cited models, not published policies.
- A `next_due_at` column is a sortable equivalent: invert the threshold, `next_due_at = last_fetched_at + g^{−1}(Λ·λ_i/w_i)/λ_i`, where Λ is the multiplier that last filled the budget; channels with `w_i/λ_i ≤ Λ` get `NULL` (never due) under binary freshness. An index on `next_due_at` makes the planning query cheap, at the cost of recomputing rows when Λ moves.
- Importance can absorb demand terms (subscriber count, language relevance, number of users following) as a product; Kolobov's experiments used a skewed 0-65535 score, so heavy-tailed weights are normal.

### Gaps
- No paper evaluates these scores on Telegram-like count observations or with a bounded per-fetch window; the window-capped variants above are untested.

## 7. Pitfalls: never-changing channels, death/removal, cold start, staleness vs cost

### Takeaway
The literature gives clear answers on starvation (binary freshness starves fast items; harmonic or smoothed estimates prevent permanent starvation), cold start (start from a prior rate, learn online, or generalise from similar items), and non-stationarity (short history windows). It says almost nothing about detecting dead or removed sources, which needs a separate rule.

### Cited Findings
- Binary freshness optimum sets `ρ*_i = 0` for every page with `μ_i/Δ_i ≤ λ`, i.e. fast-changing low-value pages are never crawled. [Azar et al.](https://www.erichorvitz.com/Crawl_1801519115.full.pdf). Kolobov et al. call this "unacceptable in practice" and the harmonic penalty fixes it. [Kolobov et al., NeurIPS 2019](https://papers.nips.cc/paper_files/paper/2019/file/ad13a2a07ca4b7642959dc0c4c740ab6-Paper.pdf)
- Kolobov et al. assume `μ, Δ > 0` "because sources that are unimportant or never change don't need to be crawled". [Kolobov et al., NeurIPS 2019](https://papers.nips.cc/paper_files/paper/2019/file/ad13a2a07ca4b7642959dc0c4c740ab6-Paper.pdf)
- A zero-observation estimate is a singularity (Eq. 11 gives Δ̂ = 0); the fix is an imaginary 0.5 observation. [Kolobov et al., NeurIPS 2019](https://papers.nips.cc/paper_files/paper/2019/file/ad13a2a07ca4b7642959dc0c4c740ab6-Paper.pdf)
- Cold start in experiments: every URL started at 1 change/day and was re-estimated daily; learning per-source rates is slow, and rates can be generalised across sources (Cho & Ntoulas sampling) or inferred from similarity (Radinsky & Bennett, WSDM 2013). [Kolobov et al., NeurIPS 2019](https://papers.nips.cc/paper_files/paper/2019/file/ad13a2a07ca4b7642959dc0c4c740ab6-Paper.pdf)
- Yandex predicts new-page quality from the quality of pages previously linked from the same source, and the optimal policy barely recrawls 70% of sources, so low-yield sources cost little even if included. [Lefortier et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1307.6080)
- Real change processes deviate from Poisson: a 24-hour periodicity spike in fast-changing pages. [Grimes & O'Brien](https://archives.iw3c2.org/www2008/papers/pdf/p1149-grimes.pdf). New-link rates vary by time of day and week. [Lefortier et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1307.6080)
- Change definitions matter: Bing counted a change only when template-extracted data changed, filtering ads and related-link lists. [Kolobov et al., SIGIR 2019](https://erichorvitz.com/SIGIR_2019_crawl_under_politeness_constraints.pdf). Olston & Pandey found churning fragments (ads) are poor crawl targets. [Olston & Pandey](https://archives.iw3c2.org/www2008/papers/pdf/p437-olstonA4.pdf)
- Staleness vs cost: Cho needs `r < 0.46` (about 2 syncs per change) for 0.8 freshness. [Cho & Garcia-Molina](https://www.kth.se/social/files/554f1d5bf27654514fd00393/synchronizing-a-database-to-improve-freshness.pdf). Yandex reached the profit upper bound at one fifth of the crawl rate BFS needed. [Lefortier et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1307.6080)
- Deterministic spacing beats Poisson-random crawling for the same rate. [Azar et al.](https://www.erichorvitz.com/Crawl_1801519115.full.pdf)

### Inferences
- Never-changing channels: under the smoothed estimator `λ̂ = (α₀ + 0)/(β₀ + T)` the rate decays like 1/T and the binary score grows like `w·λ̂·τ²/2`, so a silent channel is revisited at intervals that lengthen roughly as the square root of its silent history. That is a natural, budget-aware "stop refreshing" without a hard rule. A hard floor (for example one check per 90 days) can catch revivals.
- Death and removal are observable on Telegram (preview page missing, redirect, or channel made private) and should be a separate state machine with its own retry backoff, not a change-rate signal. A "no new posts" fetch and a "page gone" fetch must not update λ̂ the same way.
- Cold start: seed `λ̂` from a segment prior (language, subscriber bucket, discovery source) and give new channels an exploration bonus or a guaranteed early second fetch; two fetches separated by a known interval already yield an exact-count estimate, which is a big advantage over binary-observation web crawling.
- Discovery shares the budget: Yandex's ECHO is the published model for splitting one budget between recrawling sources and fetching what they reveal; in the Telegram case the analogue is splitting between refresh and discovery fetches, priced in the same units by putting both into one `ORDER BY score` with comparable value scales.

### Gaps
- I found no source on detecting source death/removal in recrawl scheduling.
- No source quantified how much a gamma prior or exploration bonus helps cold start in crawling; the Kolobov RL experiment (Fig. 3) is the closest evidence.
